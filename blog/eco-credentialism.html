Thinking · Building light

Eco-credentialism

There's a difference between being light on the land and performing it. I've tried to hold each green choice to one test: is the claim true, or have we bought it? Some choices pass. One fails. And a few passed partly because they were also the cheaper option, which I think is worth saying out loud.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

The container pool

The goods for the build come from China in a forty-foot shipping container. Our cargo fills less than half of it. Rather than pay to send the box back, the plan is to buy it outright, use it as a locked site store during the build, and then convert it into the pool. One purchase, three lives: shipping, storage, swimming. A used forty-foot box costs around US$1,100 to 1,300 in China.

Justified in eco terms: finding a use for these boxes is doing the country a favour.

I still believe that. But I'd be performing if I stopped there. The pool is also commercial. In St Andrew, properties with a pool charge a great deal more a night than those without. And the store matters as much as the pool: the old warehouse isn't weathertight until it's been worked on, so without a steel box there is nowhere dry to keep materials at the start of the job.

It's also unproven. The container walls are built to carry weight from above, not water pushing outwards, so they need welded bracing, which local welders and mechanics can do. It can never be lifted full. Every line of the cost is still an estimate, and my condition is simple: it's only worth it if we keep to the lower end. The pool is a later stage, built as the business earns, not part of the opening.

Felling two trees

The mango grove sits west of the house, and it shades the back of it. I said early on that we might have to fell a couple of trees to get light into the house. At the time that was also about solar panels on a pergola at the back, where the grove would have shaded them.

Then the panels moved to the roof, because the roof gets more reliable sun. That took away half the reason for felling. The light reason still stands, and so does the idea I like best in the whole project: mill the timber, season it, and have a local cabinetmaker build the new kitchen from it. Two mature mangoes might give enough timber for the kitchen. It's the same move Mum and Dad made with stone from the old mill.

But it's a real loss, not a free one. We're farmers in Simon. Felling a fruiting mango for cabinet doors would be wrong, so nothing comes down until two weeks of sun-mapping show which trees actually block the light. The drying clock is unforgiving too: about a year for every 25 millimetres of thickness, unless a mill will swap our green wood for seasoned. As I write, nothing has been felled.

Solar on the roof

The house will have twelve panels and two batteries on the roof, installed by a Grenadian supplier, and no electricity bill. That's a true claim. It also saves us money every month for as long as the panels last, so I won't dress it up as sacrifice.

Two candid footnotes. The business side, the kitchen in the old warehouse, has its own solar quote, and the costing also carries a backup diesel generator. A commercial kitchen can't stop when the battery runs low. And a bigger solar system made it possible to choose electric instant water heaters for the house bathroom, which is what I chose.

Gravity showers

A tank raised above an enclosure, gravity for pressure, sun for warmth. No pump, no electricity, almost no plumbing. This is the eco idea that passes the test cleanly: light touch, reversible, no concrete, local materials and local hands. It's planned for the garden side of the warehouse, and later by the pool.

Two cautions keep it honest. An open tank in the tropics breeds mosquitoes, so it must be sealed and screened. And a sun-warmed tank sits at the temperature where legionella grows, so volumes stay small and the water keeps moving. It's warmed water, not solar hot water in any official sense, and I won't sell it as that. It's also the garden shower. Inside the house, as I said, the shower heaters are electric.

The decking, where the test failed

The house and pool need about a hundred square metres of decking. The local-feeling choice was Guyana hardwood, from within the Caribbean. I chose composite boards on an aluminium frame on steel screw piles, all from China.

My reasons are real. Termites attack the frame, not just the boards, and an aluminium frame gives them nothing to eat. Nothing rots. On our desk figures it was no dearer, and probably cheaper, than hardwood. Mango from our own trees is only moderate outdoors, so it was never the answer. But I won't call composite boards shipped across the world an eco choice. It's a durability choice, made with open eyes.

What actually counts

The quieter decisions may matter more than the photogenic ones. Screw piles and compacted ground instead of poured concrete wherever we can. Keeping and restoring the furniture already in the house. Buying kitchen equipment from brands that can be serviced on the island, so nothing is thrown away in month four for want of a part. Building in stages and adding capacity when bookings arrive, not before.

None of that makes a good photo. All of it is lighter on the land.
