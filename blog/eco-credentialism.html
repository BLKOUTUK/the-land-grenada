Eco-credentialism

There's a difference between being light on the land and performing it. One test I keep coming back to is whether a green claim is true or whether we've bought it. Applied honestly, it makes most of our choices look mixed: good for some reasons, cheaper or more commercial for others, and sometimes a trade-off against something else we care about.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

The container pool

The goods for the build come from China in a forty-foot container, which our cargo fills to less than half. The idea is to buy the box rather than return it, use it as a locked store during the build, then convert it into a pool. A used forty-foot box costs about US$1,100 to 1,300 in China.

Justified in eco terms: finding a use for these boxes is doing the country a favour.

For it: reuse instead of a new concrete pool, a store we otherwise lack (the warehouse isn't weathertight until it's been worked on), and local welding work. Against it, or at least complicating it: a pool is still a pool, with a pump, water and chemicals. It's partly commercial, because properties in St Andrew with a pool charge much more a night. The walls need welded bracing, it can never be lifted full, and every cost line is still an estimate. It belongs to a later stage, built as the business earns. The idea is to go ahead only if the cost stays at the lower end of the estimate.

Felling trees for light

The mango grove shades the back of the house. Felling two trees would bring light in, and the timber could be milled and seasoned for a kitchen built by a local cabinetmaker, the same move Mum and Dad made with stone from the old mill. Two mature mangoes might give enough.

For it: daylight in a house with half-metre walls, timber from the land rather than shipped in, and local craft. Against it: we're farmers in Simon, and felling a fruiting mango is a real loss. Part of the original reason was solar panels on a rear pergola, and that fell away when the panels moved to the roof. Seasoning takes about a year per 25 millimetres unless a mill will swap green wood for seasoned. Nothing comes down until a sun-map shows which trees actually block the light. As I write, nothing has been felled.

Solar on the roof

Twelve panels and two batteries, installed by a Grenadian supplier: a house with no electricity bill. For it: clean power, resilience in a cut, lower running costs. Against, or worth saying: it is as much good economics as virtue. The panels, inverter and batteries are imported brands, not local products. The kitchen in the warehouse has its own solar quote, and its costing includes a backup diesel generator, because a commercial kitchen can't stop when the battery runs low. And a bigger system made electric instant water heaters possible in the house bathroom, which is what was chosen.

Gravity showers

A raised tank, gravity for pressure, sun for warmth: no pump and no electricity. It's planned for the garden side of the warehouse, and later by the pool. For it: about as light-touch as building gets, and local. Against it: an open tank breeds mosquitoes, so it must be sealed and screened. A sun-warmed tank sits at temperatures where legionella can grow, so volumes stay small and the water keeps moving. It's warmed water, not solar hot water in any official sense. And it serves the garden, not the house.

The decking

About a hundred square metres for the house and pool. The options were Guyana hardwood, composite boards on a hardwood frame, or composite boards on an aluminium frame with steel screw piles. The last was chosen, all from China.

For it: termites attack frames, and aluminium gives them nothing to eat; nothing rots; on desk figures it was no dearer. Against it: composite contains plastic and is shipped across the world. Hardwood from within the Caribbean is the more natural material, stays cooler underfoot, and is arguably the greener choice if it lasts. Our own mango is only moderate outdoors, so it was never really in the running. Our own notes say plainly that decking fails the "true, not bought" test.

The quieter choices

Some of the lightest decisions are the least visible: screw piles instead of poured concrete, restoring the furniture already in the house, buying kitchen equipment that can be serviced on the island, and adding guest rooms only when bookings justify them. They're less photogenic, and they aren't free of trade-offs either. Screw piles are steel. Restored furniture may not suit guests.

Where things stand

Decided so far: roof solar, electric heaters in the house, imported decking on aluminium. Planned but not built: the container pool, the gravity showers, the mango kitchen. Each could still change. A welder's real price could sink the pool. A sun-map could spare the trees. A hardwood quote could reopen the decking. I'd rather describe each honestly than call the whole project "eco".
