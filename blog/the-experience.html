Thinking · Staying here

The experience

What should it feel like to stay at the land? I want to answer that carefully, because none of it is built yet. The plan is to build from February 2027, hold a trial in June, and open for the carnival season in July. So this is what we are aiming for, not a brochure.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

The place first

The land is seven acres at Simon, in the parish of St Andrew, on Grenada's east coast. It is farmed land with a mill ruin and a public footpath, not resort land. The sea is a seven-minute walk from the house, not a view from it. The view runs west, to the inland hills, and the sunset comes down that line. The trade wind comes in from the east-north-east.

This short film walks the site plan: the house, the old warehouse that becomes the kitchen, and the mango grove where the suites will go.

Two ways to stay

The whole house. Mum and Dad's stone house is let as one booking, to one party, usually two couples travelling together. You have the whole house to yourselves: the open-plan kitchen and dining room, the arched verandah, the roof terrace, and the back of the house opened up to the garden onto a deck. Nobody you don't know is sharing it with you.

A suite in the mango grove. Three suites sit round a shared yard under the trees, each with its own bathroom and a glass front. Each takes a couple, a family of four, or six with the sofa bed. Couples for most of the year, I imagine, and families at Carnival. A team or a residential course can take the whole grove, with the kitchen. The first two suites come with the opening, and the third follows as the business earns. Right now they are drawings and a concept film.

What I want the grove to feel like is under-tree, shaded, dark timber, nothing built up and nothing that looks like a beach resort. And no cameras in the yards. A place to rest should feel unwatched.

The table

Everything turns on one kitchen, in the old fieldstone warehouse, built to Ministry of Health standards, with a servery to the garden and a takeaway window to the road.

Friday is Lunch & Lime: our own chef, a set menu of things like an elevated oil-down, oxtail stew and a seasonal fish course. It's aimed at the kind of lunch nobody at a roadside stall can host: a leaving do, an office birthday, a networking lunch. Lunch comes first. Dinner follows only if people ask for it.

Then the supper clubs, with visiting chefs. The chef brings the menu and sets the price; we bring the kitchen, the verandah and about thirty seats. The menu gets written after the harvest, not before. On a clear evening, six people can take sunset drinks on the roof terrace before dinner.

If everyone came we'd have a terrible time. I need thirty diners. The club element of the supper club is key.

The food I want is traditional Caribbean cooking done well, with more fish and vegetables and less that's fried. That's not a health-food fashion. It reflects real concern about diet and health in Grenada.

The register

Simon already has its evenings. The Friday night club by the nearest beach is loud enough to hear from the balcony, and there's a sports bar by the Board Bridge. We aren't trying to compete with either. What the neighbourhood doesn't have is a comfortable, well-lit evening built around artists, women, dining, live music, the diaspora and culture, under fairy lights. That's the gap we're aiming at. Once a month there's also an idea for a quiet midweek gathering for the UK diaspora, with anglophiles welcome.

Seamoon, Simon and Carnival

Simon is busier than it looks. Seamoon Industrial Park is in the village itself, and a call centre there employs around 800 people. A FIFA-funded football development centre is being built. Seamoon Cultural Centre is just along the road. And every year the Seamoon Pavilion hosts the Soca Monarch semi-finals, which puts Simon on the national Carnival map. Spicemas falls on 9 and 10 August in 2027, and that's the season we're opening for.

To get around, there will be electric scooters and trikes. On the public road they count as motorcycles, so guests without the right licence ride them on the land only. Further afield: Bathway and Levera, Grenville market and the Tivoli Drummers.

What isn't here yet

To be plain: nothing above is built. The pool, a converted shipping container, is a later stage. The firepit, film nights and the bar annex wait until there are guests for them. We still need a liquor licence, and nobody has yet asked a Grenadian what they would pay for a supper-club seat, which is the cheapest piece of research we haven't done. The prices and the programme will change as we learn.

What won't change is the idea: a place people book in order to be together, for a reason five minutes down the road, which happens to have beds.
