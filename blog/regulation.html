Regulation

Nobody films the licence applications. But what it takes to do this legally in Grenada has shaped the plan as much as any drawing. Here is the map as I understand it in October 2026: what's settled, what's asked, and what isn't answered yet.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

Why start with the rules

When the plan was still safari tents in the garden, we asked whether tents might fall outside the rules because the rules don't mention them. They don't fall outside. In Grenada, anything that counts as tourist accommodation needs a licence; there is no lawful unlicensed version. That settled my approach: talk to the regulators before building, not after, and design to their answers. I'd rather ask an awkward question now than pull down a wall later.

The Tourism Authority

The Grenada Tourism Authority licenses lets. In June they told us that one licence covers every unit on one compound under one operator. They also gave us their definitions. A villa has at least three bedrooms, is run by a company or agent, employs at least three people and is available nine months a year. A guest house has three or more let bedrooms. Our house isn't a villa in that sense, and we haven't asked for it to be licensed as one.

On 7 October I sent them the whole plan in one pack: the house let whole, the kitchen in the old warehouse, three suites in the mango grove, and me living on site. I asked whether one licence could cover it; whether they would accept the two house rooms, which are just under the 23.2 square metre bedroom standard; whether part-year letting fits; and how the standard applies to a suite.

They replied on 12 October. One licence for the whole place is possible if it is run as one business to the public. The house can be a family home for part of the year and let for the rest, with changeover steps such as removing personal belongings. The undersized rooms and the suites go to the Planning and Development Authority, and the Tourism Authority's inspectors will follow its advice.

Planning comes first

That made planning the step everything waits for. The Planning and Development Authority's approval is required before construction or major conversion, and it covers the house conversion, the two smaller bedrooms and the suites. I've decided we get planning approval before we place the order for anything from China, using proper drawings developed with the factories. It's slower, but committing money to buildings we can't use would be worse. It puts planning ahead of the end-of-November order date, and it could move the timetable.

There is also no single wind speed written into Grenada's building code. It adopts the OECS Building Code, which defers to an older Caribbean code and allows another recognised standard with the Director's approval. So every supplier has to tell us which standard their product meets, and we put that in front of the engineer and the Authority.

Fire, food and drink

The Tourism Authority also pointed us to the Fire Division, for fire safety, emergency access and suppression in the final designs. One question I've already flagged is the roof terrace, which is reached by a single open stair.

The kitchen is designed to Ministry of Health standards: a grease trap, handwashing separate from food prep, food-safe finishes, fly screens, extraction and fire suppression. Environmental health will look at wastewater and grease traps. Everyone who works in the kitchen will need food-handler training and clearance.

Drink was the gap in our thinking for longest. Serving food and alcohol together needs two licences, a liquor licence and a refreshment house licence. They go through Inland Revenue, then environmental health and police checks, then Cabinet, which takes weeks. The standard liquor permit for a place outside town is modest, and applicants need letters of recommendation from existing licence-holders in the district. There is also an occasional licence that may suit trading a few days a week better. Its fee isn't published, and the sources disagree on which office issues which licence. The fees I've seen come from older sources, so they need confirming.

Power and structure

The Electrical Inspectorate signs off the new wiring in the house and the warehouse, and the grid company requires an external isolator that locks off, so the solar system can be made safe. The structural engineer is the biggest unknown in the house: the cracks, how much load the roof terrace can take, and anything we put at height. One firm has already declined. We need that visit.

Duty relief

Grenada's investment agency, GIDC, offers relief on import duty for tourism investment. We've applied. On 7 October they told us a detailed review was under way and a call would follow. As I understand their guide, the certificate is issued to the project against a list of goods and applied by Customs at import, so it has to exist before our container clears in February. We don't have an answer yet. GIDC's offices are at Seamoon, in Simon itself, which helps.

Who owns what

The family's plan is two companies: one that holds the house, and mine, which runs the kitchen and suites and leases what it uses. Non-citizens need a government licence to hold land in Grenada, and that applies to a company whose shares are mostly held by non-citizens. My parents are Grenadian, and I'm applying for citizenship by descent. Until it comes through, orders go in my name and move into the companies once they exist. A Grenadian lawyer will confirm all of this before anything is set up.

What this adds up to

If the build finishes in May, the sign-offs follow straight after: electrics, health, the Tourism Authority's inspection, the drink licences, insurance, and registering the e-scooters, which count as motorcycles on a public road. None of this is glamorous, and plenty of it is still unanswered. But each answer so far has made the plan clearer, and none has been a no.
