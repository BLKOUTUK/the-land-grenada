Thinking · Doing it properly

Regulation

What it takes to do this legally in Grenada has shaped the plan as much as any drawing. There are choices here as well as rules: how early to ask, which route to take, and how much to hold up the build for an answer. This is the map as of October 2026, and the trade-offs in it.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

Ask early, or build and then ask?

When the plan was still safari tents in the garden, we asked whether tents might fall outside the rules because the rules don't mention them. They don't: anything that counts as tourist accommodation needs a licence. That left a choice of approach. Talking to regulators before building means designing to their answers and avoiding expensive changes. It also means waiting for replies, making our plans visible early, and inviting questions we might never have been asked. I chose to ask first, but it costs time.

The Tourism Authority

In June the Grenada Tourism Authority told us that one licence covers every unit on a compound under one operator, and gave us their definitions. A villa needs at least three bedrooms, management by a company or agent, three staff and nine months' availability a year. A guest house has three or more let bedrooms. Neither fits our house neatly.

On 7 October I sent them the whole plan and asked direct questions. They replied on 12 October. One licence for the whole place is possible if it's run as one business to the public. The house can be a family home for part of the year and let for the rest, with changeover steps. The two let bedrooms, just under the 23.2 square metre standard, and the suites go to the Planning and Development Authority, and the Tourism Authority's inspectors will follow its advice.

One licence for everything is simpler. It also ties the house and the business together in the regulator's eyes, at the same time as the family is talking about keeping them in two separate companies. How those two fit together is still to be worked out.

Planning first, or order first?

Planning approval is required before construction or major conversion. I've decided to get it before ordering anything from China, using drawings developed with the factories. For: we never pay for buildings we can't use. Against: it puts planning ahead of the end-of-November order date, and if planning is slow, the goods miss the January sailing before Chinese New Year and the opening slips by around two months.

Grenada's building code also has no single wind speed of its own. It adopts the OECS Building Code, which defers to an older Caribbean code and allows another recognised standard with the Director's approval. That gives us flexibility. It also means more judgement calls, and more depends on the engineer.

Fire, food and drink

The Fire Division will review fire safety, emergency access and suppression. The roof terrace is reached by a single open stair, which may be a question. The kitchen is designed to Ministry of Health standards, environmental health will look at wastewater and grease traps, and kitchen staff need food-handler training.

Selling food and alcohol together needs both a liquor licence and a refreshment house licence, through Inland Revenue, then environmental health and police checks, then Cabinet. That takes weeks. An occasional licence might suit trading a few days a week better, but its fee isn't published, and sources disagree on which office handles which licence. The fees I've seen come from older sources. Applicants also need letters of recommendation from existing licence-holders nearby. That's a nice local check, and also a dependence on the goodwill of businesses we'd partly compete with.

Duty relief: worth the wait?

Grenada's investment agency, GIDC, offers relief on import duty for tourism investment, and we've applied. For: real savings on imported goods. Against: as I read their guide, the certificate must exist before our container clears in February, and on 7 October they said only that a detailed review was under way. There are mixed readings, too, of what the relief covers. Planning on relief we don't get would leave a gap; planning without it means carrying a cost we may not need.

Power, structure and ownership

The Electrical Inspectorate signs off the wiring, and the grid company requires a lockable external isolator for the solar. The structural engineer, who still has to visit, holds the biggest unknown in the house: the cracks and the roof terrace. One firm has already declined.

Non-citizens need a government licence to hold land in Grenada, and that applies to a company mostly held by non-citizens. My parents are Grenadian, and I'm applying for citizenship by descent. Until it comes through, orders would go in my name and move into the companies later. That's practical, but it puts a lot in one person's name for a while. A Grenadian lawyer will confirm the structure before anything is set up.

Where things stand

Answered: the Tourism Authority's broad position. Waiting: planning, GIDC, the engineer, Fire, the drink licences, legal advice. Decided: ask before building, and get planning before ordering. What would change things: a planning refusal on the bedrooms or suites, a GIDC no, or legal advice that the two-company structure doesn't work as planned. Any of those would reshape the plan rather than simply delay it.
