Grenada's online culture

A film series and a small place at Simon only work if the right people find them. That depends on how Grenadians, at home and abroad, actually find, share and talk online. I'm still learning this, and the choices each have a cost.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

What the numbers say

DataReportal's latest Grenada figures put about 87,000 people online at the end of 2025, roughly three in four of the population, with around 30,000 still offline. Facebook's advertising reach was about 67,000 people, Instagram's about 56,000, TikTok's about 47,000 adults, and X's under 15,000. Those are advertising estimates, not counts of active people, so they're a rough guide rather than a census. I couldn't find a published survey of WhatsApp use in Grenada. Regional app charts have long put it near the top of downloads, but downloads aren't use, so I won't put a number on it.

The news landscape is a mix of online papers such as NOW Grenada and The New Today, broadcasters like GBN, radio stations that stream online, and a government information service that now does a weekly news wrap-up. A lot of it travels by being shared on Facebook and WhatsApp rather than through people visiting the sites directly.

What our own record shows

Our evidence is small but telling. In early October the YouTube channel had three subscribers and about 1,200 views in total. Vertical short cuts outperformed the full episodes by about three to one. The trailer drew 33 views in its first month with no announcement at all, and we still don't know where they came from, because we haven't switched on the analytics that would tell us. My personal Facebook has been the best channel so far, partly because that's where family networks live. Facebook posts from a personal profile can't be automated, so they're done by hand.

Facebook is also where much of the island's commerce happens in public. When I looked for agents linking China and Grenada, Facebook posts turned them up where the wider web didn't. A local supplier of suite-style units advertises on social media. A restaurant one village over takes orders by WhatsApp, and delivery platforms already list Grenadian restaurants.

Reach against intimacy

YouTube and Instagram offer reach: a short film can travel to people we'll never meet. For the businesses, though, what matters is particular people with a reason to be in Simon: a returning family, a football squad, a team from the industrial park. Our plan says a thousand of the right viewers are worth more than a hundred thousand of the wrong ones. The case for chasing reach is that it compounds and costs us nothing per view. The case against is that it rewards what spreads, not what's true or useful to Simon, and it pulls the films towards spectacle.

WhatsApp is the opposite. It's intimate, trusted and forwarded inside families and village groups. The plan is a WhatsApp list, with each film carrying a link that tells us which film brought someone in, and a QR code at the gate. For: it reaches local people who will never fill in a form. Against: it doesn't scale, it depends on my phone and my time, and private messages are hard to measure honestly.

Platform dependence

Almost everything here sits on platforms owned elsewhere. Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp are one company; YouTube is another. They can change the rules, the feed or the price at any time, and a small place has no say. The alternative is our own website and our own list, which we control but which nobody finds without the platforms. In practice it's both: use the platforms to be found, and move people we've met onto something we control. That move is harder than it sounds.

Diaspora against local

The diaspora, in the UK, New York, New Jersey and Toronto, is reachable online, plans trips home for Christmas, Carnival and the summer, and is most likely to book the whole house. Local people in Simon and St Andrew are reached by WhatsApp, Facebook and word of mouth at the gate, and they're who the Friday lunch and the supper clubs depend on. Films made for one audience can miss the other. A returner's nostalgia isn't a neighbour's everyday life, and local prices aren't diaspora prices. We plan to ask both groups, by WhatsApp poll and YouTube poll, what they'd pay. Nobody has asked yet.

Where things stand

Decided so far: release the finished episodes one a week from this autumn, each aimed at a named audience; lead with short vertical cuts; use WhatsApp as the next step; and keep this personal, not a marketing campaign. Unknown: where our viewers come from, whether films turn into bookings, and how local people feel about being filmed and shown. What would change it: real traffic data, a WhatsApp list that grows or doesn't, and what the first local diners tell us about how they heard.
