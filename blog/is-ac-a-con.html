Is AC a con?

From the first brief, the plan for the land has said no air-conditioning, passive cooling only. Someone in the family asked me, fairly, why we were budgeting for solar and not for AC. It deserves a proper answer, which means setting out both sides.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

First, they aren't either-or

Solar and AC answer different questions. Solar is the power supply: no electricity bill, and power that rides out a cut. AC is something that uses power. The house solar is a quote from Mr Smith, a Grenadian installer in Grand Anse: twelve panels on the roof, a hybrid inverter and two batteries, installed. The real question is whether the house should be cooled by its design, by machines, or by some of each.

The case for AC

It's hot at Simon: 26 to 32 degrees, at 80 to 90 per cent humidity, all year. Many visitors expect a cool bedroom as standard. The one licensed glampsite on the Tourism Authority's own site lists AC first among its features, ahead of breakfast and wifi. There is already an old unit on the kitchen wall of the house. Comfort affects whether people sleep, whether they come back, and what they say about us. A guest who has paid for Carnival week and can't sleep won't be consoled by a design principle. And the suites in the mango grove are insulated modules. One reason I listed for choosing them over tents was that they are rooms that lock, insulate and can take AC.

The case for passive cooling

The house was built for this climate. Half-metre stone walls hold the cool. Ceiling fans move air. Louvre windows let the breeze through: a louvre passes about 85 per cent of its area as open air, against about 48 per cent for an awning window. Bahama shutters shade the glass. Skylights that open at the top let hot air out, with the stairwell working as a chimney. The trade wind comes from the east-north-east.

Then there's the energy. When we sized solar for a small home on the land, the daily load was about 11 kilowatt-hours with no AC. One split unit adds three to six a day: roughly two more kilowatts of panels and five more kilowatt-hours of battery, about £2,000, before buying the unit. AC doesn't sit on top of a solar design. It changes it.

And there's damp. In 90 per cent humidity, a room cooled some of the time can be worse than one never cooled. Chill it while guests are in, close it up when they leave, and you risk condensation on cold surfaces and mould behind the wardrobe.

The weak points on each side

Passive cooling has a hard afternoon. The view runs west, to the hills and the sunset, and that's where the sun beats down from two to six. If the shading isn't right, with deep reveals, shutters on the west glass, a shade pergola and trees, the house is uncomfortable in the afternoon. Passive design also depends on the breeze, and on guests who are happy to live with open shutters and moving air. Not everyone is.

AC's weak points are cost, power, damp and noise, and a building that slowly gets designed around the machine rather than the climate. Once a cooled bedroom is what we sell, it's hard to go back.

Where things stand

What's decided so far: no AC is budgeted for the house, and the design aims to cool it without machines. What isn't decided: whether that holds for every room and every guest. The design rules we set for new buildings carry conduit for a split unit, as provision rather than something installed by default. Bedroom AC could be added later, and the solar would help to run it. The suites are a separate question and genuinely open.

What would change it? Guests telling us, during the trial and the first season, that the house is too hot to sleep in. Afternoon temperatures inside the house once the shading is in. And what the market expects: if visitors to this coast consistently won't book without AC, that's information, not an insult. I'd rather find out from people staying in the house than decide it from a spreadsheet in London.
