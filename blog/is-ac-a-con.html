Is AC a con?

From the very first brief, the plan for the land has said the same thing: no air-conditioning, passive cooling only. Someone in the family asked me, fairly, why we were paying for solar and not for AC. Here is the thinking, both sides of it.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

The honest case for AC

Let me start with the other side, because it's strong. It is hot at Simon: 26 to 32 degrees, with humidity at 80 to 90 per cent, every day of the year. Plenty of visitors expect a cool bedroom as a matter of course. The one licensed glampsite listed on the Tourism Authority's own site puts AC first in its list of features, ahead of breakfast and wifi. There is even an old unit on the kitchen wall of the house already. If a couple has paid for Carnival week and can't sleep, no design principle of mine is going to console them.

So I don't think AC is a con. I think it is often a crutch for buildings that weren't designed for where they stand. The question is whether ours can be.

Solar and AC aren't alternatives

First, the question itself. Solar and AC aren't either-or. Solar is the power supply: no electricity bill, and power that rides out a cut. AC is something that uses power. The house system is quoted by Mr Smith, a Grenadian installer in Grand Anse: twelve panels on the roof, a hybrid inverter and two batteries, installed. Whatever we decide about cooling, the house needs that.

How the house cools itself

The design does the work instead. Mum and Dad's half-metre stone walls hold the cool. Ceiling fans move the air. Louvre windows let the breeze through: a louvre passes about 85 per cent of its area as open air, against about 48 per cent for an awning window, which is why we chose louvres. Bahama shutters shade the glass and double as storm protection. And skylights that open at the top let hot air leave, with the stairwell working as a chimney. That last point is one reason the skylights survived every attempt to cut them.

It is the old way of building in the Caribbean, and it is how the trade wind from the east-north-east is meant to be used.

What AC would do to the numbers

When we sized solar for a small home on the land, the whole daily load came to about 11 kilowatt-hours, with no AC. One split unit adds three to six kilowatt-hours a day on its own. Covering that means roughly two more kilowatts of panels and five more kilowatt-hours of battery, about £2,000 extra, before you buy the unit. AC doesn't sit on top of a solar design. It rewrites it.

The damp problem

This is the argument that persuaded me most, and it isn't about money. In 90 per cent humidity, a room that is cooled some of the time is worse than one that is never cooled. Chill a room while guests are in, shut it up when they leave, and you get condensation on cold surfaces and mould behind the wardrobe. The most common mould complaint in the Caribbean is the back of a fitted wardrobe on an outside wall. A house that breathes all the time stays drier than one that is sealed and chilled on and off.

Where it gets hard: the afternoon

The view from the house runs west, to the inland hills and the sunset. That is also where the sun beats down between two and six in the afternoon. Get the shading wrong and the house is unusable in the afternoon, someone puts in AC, the solar sizing breaks, and the whole approach unravels. So the shading has to be designed in, not hoped for: deep reveals and shutters on the west glass, a shade pergola wrapping the back of the house, and trees. It costs little if it is done from the start, and a lot if it isn't.

The suites are a different case

The guest suites in the mango grove are insulated, steel-framed modules, not the old stone house. When I switched from safari tents to these suites, one of the things I listed in their favour was simple: they are rooms that lock, insulate and can take AC. That is a door I've deliberately left open, not a decision already made.

Where I've landed

No AC is budgeted in the house. The design cools itself, with stone, fans, louvres, shutters and the stack effect. But it isn't dogma. The design rules we set for new buildings on the land carry conduit for a split unit as insurance: provision, never installed by default. If bedroom AC proves to be needed, it can be added, and the solar would help to run it.

I'd rather start from a building that works with its climate and add a machine if we must, than start with the machine and never find out. If our first guests tell us we've got it wrong, we'll listen.
