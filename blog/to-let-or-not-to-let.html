To let or not to let

My parents' house at Simon could go one of two ways. It could be a private family home, or it could be a house that pays its way. This is how I've been weighing it, and where I've landed for now.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

What we're deciding about

Mum and Dad built the house from stone that was already on the land, salvaged from the old water mill and the Georgian houses at the heart of the village. The walls are half a metre thick. A hurricane hit midway through the build, and they carried on anyway.

Today it stands empty, and the damp, the cracks and the weather are winning. It is sound in its bones and failing everywhere else. Left as it is, the realistic end is a sale. So the question isn't really whether to do something. It's what shape the house should be in once we have.

Two shapes, over twenty years

When I set this out for the family, I drew it as two shapes.

The first is a private home. We make the house sound: roof, cracks, rewire, plumbing, septic, windows, shutters. The kitchen and the finishes only if the money stretches. Then, for most of the year, it sits empty, and the upkeep, the insurance and the storm repairs come out of our own pockets, every year, with nothing coming back in.

The second is a house that pays its way. The house is finished properly: kitchen, bathroom, solar on the roof, the back opened up to the land, decks. Someone lives in it and is on the ground. For part of the year it's let, and the letting carries the costs. Further down the line it builds a fund for what the family will need next: care as we get older, a place the next generation can use and look after, and bursaries for young people in Simon.

Both keep the house in the family. The question is which one still looks after it in twenty years.

The case for keeping it private

I don't think the private-home shape is wrong, and it has been put to me, fairly, from within the family. It's simpler. It takes the licensing questions off the table entirely, including the awkward one about bedroom sizes. Those are real advantages, and I'd rather name them than argue past them.

What worries me is the empty part. An empty house in that climate is exactly what has been killing this one: damp, termites, bush through the windows. St Andrew is a wet parish, with two to two and a half metres of rain a year and humidity that sits at 80 to 90 per cent. A house there needs someone to open it up, chase the engineer, drop the shutters when a storm is named and catch the leak before it becomes a ceiling. A house that is lived in and used does better than one that is visited.

Whole house, never room by room

If we let it, how matters as much as whether. I've been firm on one thing from the start: the house is let as one whole house, to one party, typically two couples travelling together. It is never let room by room, so no guest ever shares the house, or its bathroom, with a stranger.

That's partly about feel. A family house shouldn't turn into a guest house with a lock on every door. It's also about the rules. Grenada's Tourism Authority expects a let bedroom to be at least 23.2 square metres, 250 square feet. The two rooms we would let are Bedroom C, at 23.0, and the studio, at 21.4. Both just under. The other two bedrooms are a long way under. When we ran the arithmetic on letting rooms one by one, the whole house could not yield three rooms of that size, wherever the walls went. Letting it whole, to one party, is the only version that makes sense of the building we actually have.

What the Tourism Authority said

On 7 October I sent the Grenada Tourism Authority the whole plan and asked straight questions. They answered on 12 October. One licence can cover the whole place if it is run as one business to the public. The house can be a family home for part of the year and let for the rest, with changeover steps in between, such as clearing personal belongings. The two let bedrooms and the guest suites go to the Planning and Development Authority, which is a required step before building. Nothing in that reply says no. It does mean more paperwork before anyone picks up a tool.

The carnival weeks

Here is the tension I feel most. The weeks that let best, Carnival and the summer, are exactly the weeks many of us would want to be there. Spicemas falls on 9 and 10 August next year.

I think we can have both. At Carnival the house is let to two couples in the two largest rooms. The two smaller bedrooms stay free for family, two of us in each. Two more can stay with me in my suite in the grove, because whenever the house is let, I move out of it. That's six of us at Carnival without getting in the way of a booking. And if family encourage people they know to book the house, so much the better.

We're expecting lets of around twenty weeks a year, mostly in that peak. The rest of the year, the house is the family's.

Where it stands

Nothing is decided. It's a family house and a family decision, and we're talking it through this autumn. My own view is that the house that pays its way is the one most likely to still be standing, and still ours, when the next generation comes to look after it. But I'd rather we chose that together, with the trade-offs in plain sight, than drifted into it.
