Hurricane, and protection from the elements

Building in St Andrew means building for storms, damp, termites, salt and sun at once. Every protection costs money, and some cost comfort or looks too. Here are the choices, what each buys, what it costs, and what is still unconfirmed.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

The background

Hurricane Ivan struck Grenada on 7 September 2004. It damaged nearly nine homes in ten on the island and destroyed most of the nutmeg trees. A hurricane hit midway through the building of our house too, and Mum and Dad carried on, with salvaged stone and half-metre walls.

How strong is strong enough?

There are two honest ways to frame this. One is to aim for hurricane-proof: design to the highest wind rating available and pay for it. The other, which I've used so far, treats it as harm reduction. A direct hit from a Category 4 or 5 storm isn't survivable for buildings at our budget, so we build sensibly and let insurance carry the worst case.

Harm reduction keeps costs within reach. It also leaves more to insurance and to luck, and it's a judgement other people might reasonably make differently, especially for guests sleeping in the buildings.

Part of the difficulty is that Grenada's code has no wind speed of its own. It adopts the OECS Building Code, which defers to an older Caribbean code and allows another recognised standard with the Director's approval. So we ask each supplier which standard their product meets, and price any upgrade as a separate line. We've made mistakes here: an early figure of 240 km/h in our factory requests came from our own research, not a regulator, and three factories withdrew before we corrected it. Storm "grades" on the Beaufort scale, around 100 km/h at the top, aren't hurricane ratings at all.

The suite factory we're furthest along with indicates about 118 km/h as standard and about 202 km/h upgraded. That's not a formal offer, and the standard behind it still needs confirming. Whether the upgrade is worth its cost is an open choice.

Tents or hard shells

We first planned safari tents. They were cheaper, lighter and easy to add one at a time. But they need a trained crew, a three-to-five-day take-down drill before a storm and a dry store for the fabric, and they're exposed whenever a storm passes. Hard-shell suites on screw piles stay up and need no drill, but they cost more and offer fewer beds. We switched to the suites. The deciding detail now is how well they're tied down, from roof to ground.

The house

The roof is to be repaired and coated rather than replaced. That's cheaper, but it relies on a structure the engineer still has to inspect. Bahama shutters on every window act as the storm skin. They're traditional and effective, but they need someone to close them. The new five-metre opening at the back is a hurricane-rated slider: much dearer than standard glazing, but standard glazing wouldn't satisfy an insurer or the inspection.

Solar panels on a pergola could be unbolted and stored before a named storm. On the roof they can't, and they need sealed fixings through the sheet. We chose the roof for more reliable sun, accepting the storm exposure.

Damp, termites, salt and sun

Every-day weather may do more harm than storms. At 80 to 90 per cent humidity, stone walls need to breathe: limewash rather than cement tanking. Termites leave steel and aluminium alone, so the risk is in the timber we add, and nothing timber should touch the ground. Salt air means galvanised or marine-grade fixings everywhere, which costs more up front and less over ten years. The west sun needs shutters, deep reveals and shade. Each measure has a cost, and none of them are visible in a photograph.

Timing and insurance

The build is timed for the dry season, February to May, finishing before the hurricane season starts on 1 June. That's tight: a slip in shipping pushes work into the season. Insurance isn't priced yet. Caribbean policies usually carry a named-storm excess of 2 to 5 per cent of the sum insured. Insurers are wary of unreinforced masonry, so the engineer's report will matter. Many also exclude or load houses left empty for more than a month or two.

Someone on the ground

The strongest protection may be a person: someone living there who closes the shutters, chases the engineer and catches the small leak. That's the plan for the first five years or so, with me in residence. Its strength is attention. Its weakness is dependence on one person, and the question of what happens when I'm away.

Where things stand

Decided: harm reduction rather than hurricane-proofing, hard-shell suites, roof solar, shutters, a build in the dry season. Unconfirmed: the suites' wind rating and its standard, the engineer's findings, insurance terms and premiums. Any of those could change the plan. An engineer's report showing the roof structure is weak, or an insurer refusing cover, would matter more than any product choice.
