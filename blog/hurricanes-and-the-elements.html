Hurricane, and protection from the elements

Building in St Andrew means building for storms, damp, termites, salt and sun, all at once. Here is how we're thinking about each, and where the answers are still not in.

Rob Berkeley · October 2026

A house that already knows about hurricanes

Hurricane Ivan struck Grenada on 7 September 2004. It damaged nearly nine homes in ten on the island and destroyed most of the nutmeg that gave the Spice Isle its name. A hurricane hit midway through the building of our house, and Mum and Dad carried on anyway, with stone salvaged from the old mill and the Georgian houses in the village. The half-metre walls are their answer to the weather, and the cracks where different stones meet are part of the story too.

Harm reduction, not hurricane-proofing

I want to be straight about the limit first. A direct hit from a Category 4 or 5 hurricane is not survivable for any of the buildings we can afford, and no supplier at this budget would claim otherwise. So this is about reducing harm, not about being hurricane-proof. The insurance is what carries the worst case, and that changes how we think about everything else.

What wind are we designing for?

Less settled than you might think. Grenada's building code has no wind speed of its own. It adopts the OECS Building Code, which defers to an older Caribbean code and lets the Director accept another recognised standard, such as the American ASCE 7. So there is no single number to design to. Our approach is to ask every supplier which standard their product meets, on what basis, and what an upgrade costs as a separate line.

We learned this the hard way. Early in the search, a figure of 240 km/h crept into our own requests to factories. It came from our research, not from any regulator. We withdrew it, but not before three factories had walked away. We also learned not to accept storm "grades" on the Beaufort scale, which top out around 100 km/h and are not hurricane ratings at all.

For the guest suites, the factory we're furthest along with has given a standard rating of about 118 km/h and an upgraded rating of about 202 km/h. That's an indication, not a formal offer, and the standard behind those figures still has to be confirmed. The detail that matters most is unglamorous: how the roof is tied down to the ground, all the way through. A strong box on weak anchors is a kite.

The house

The roof is being repaired and coated rather than replaced; it's corrugated and sound. A structural engineer still has to look at the cracks and at how much load the roof terrace can take before anything is closed up, and that visit is the biggest unknown in the house budget. Every window gets Bahama shutters as its storm skin, so the glass doesn't have to do that job. The big new opening at the back is a five-metre, hurricane-rated sliding door.

The solar panels go on the roof. We considered putting them on a pergola, partly because panels at pergola height can be unbolted and stored before a named storm. We chose the roof for more reliable sun, which means sealed fixings through the roof sheet and keeping clear of the skylights.

The suites, the decks and the pool

We first planned safari tents in the grove. I moved to hard-shell suites partly because a hard shell stays up. A tent needs a trained crew, a three-to-five-day warning drill and somewhere dry to store the fabric, and it is exposed whenever a storm passes. A steel suite on screw piles needs none of that.

The decks sit on steel screw piles with aluminium frames: no slab, no timber touching the ground. A shade pergola on the warehouse side is designed to come down before a storm. The pool, a converted shipping container, can never be lifted full, so it sits on a prepared base and its walls need welded bracing.

Damp, termites, salt and sun

Hurricanes get the headlines, but every-day weather does more damage. The air sits at 80 to 90 per cent humidity. The stone walls need to breathe: limewash, not cement tanking, which was the wrong answer for a wall like this. Termites are a fact of life here. Steel and aluminium are immune, so the risk sits in the timber we add, which is why nothing timber touches the ground and every underfloor space is ventilated and open to inspection. Salt in the air means galvanised or marine-grade fixings everywhere. And the west sun in the afternoon is handled with shutters, deep reveals and shade.

Timing and insurance

The build is timed for the dry season, February to May, so the main work finishes before the hurricane season starts on 1 June. A storm plan has to be live by the trial. Insurance is still to be priced. Caribbean policies usually carry a named-storm excess of 2 to 5 per cent of the sum insured. Insurers are wary of unreinforced masonry, and the engineer's report will double as our evidence. And they often exclude, or charge more for, houses left empty for more than a month or two.

Why someone on the ground matters most

That last point brings me back to the house. What has been damaging it isn't one storm. It is emptiness: damp, termites, bush through the windows. The best protection we can give it isn't a product. It's someone living there, who meets the builder, chases the engineer, drops the shutters when a storm is named and notices the small leak before it becomes a big one. For the first five years, more or less, that's me.
