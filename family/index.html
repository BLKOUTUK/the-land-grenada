A proposal to the family · September 2026

The house at Simon

Dad built it from stone that was already on the land — salvaged from the old water mill and the Georgian houses at the heart of the village. The walls are half a metre thick. The hurricane hit midway through the build and he carried on anyway. It is standing empty now, and the damp, the cracks and the weather are winning.

What I'm asking for

One ask, once
£90,000
This restores the house Dad built, and keeps it in the family.

It buys the house. Nothing else. Not a business, not a venture, not a share of anything I might make out there. A share of the house Dad built, brought back properly, and kept in the family.

What that actually means: a working house in Grenada that's yours to use — not a preserved shell nobody visits, but a base for holidays, for you, your own family, the wider family, and the friends you'd bring. That's what the roof, the rewire and the two bathrooms are actually for.

Split six ways — Mum, Dad, and the four of us, equal shares — that's £15,000 each, once. After that there is nothing further to find, ever — no running costs, no maintenance calls, no second round. I cover all of that. More on why below.

What it does not buy — and why that matters

I am going to run something there. A kitchen in the old warehouse, supper clubs, a takeaway window on the road, artists' workshops in the house, and in time some tents in the garden.

None of that is in this ask, and none of it is yours. I pay for all of it. If it works I keep what it makes; if it fails I carry the loss on my own.

I've set it up that way deliberately, for one reason: it means you don't have to believe any of it to say yes. If I'd asked you to fund a business you'd be entitled to interrogate my numbers, and my numbers are honestly a bit thin — I've never run a supper club and nobody has asked a single Grenadian what they'd pay for one. You'd be right to be sceptical. So I've taken that out of your decision entirely.

The one thing I owe the house

From year three the business pays rent to the house, as a share of what it takes rather than a fixed sum — so it pays little in a thin year and properly in a good one. That money is the house's, not mine.

I'd rather set that now, in writing, than have anyone wonder in ten years whether my trading was quietly subsidised by their fifteen thousand.

That's four to seven times what any one of you is being asked for, and all of the risk. I'm not saying it to be noble about it — I'm saying it because it's the honest answer to the question of whether this is fair, and you shouldn't have to work it out for yourselves.

I'll be living there

Years one to five, more or less. You should know that going in, and I want to say it plainly rather than have it surface later as something I skated over.

Put the other way round: an empty house in that climate is what's killing this one. Damp, termites, bush coming in through the windows. Someone living in it and maintaining it isn't a favour I'm asking for — it's the thing the £90,000 is buying. It also means there's someone on the ground to meet the builder, chase the engineer and take the tarpaulins down when a storm is named.

It's the family's house throughout, and it's there for all of us to use.

What I don't know yet

I'd rather put this in writing now than have it arrive as a surprise later.

  • The structural engineer hasn't been. What the cracks need is the biggest number still moving. The contingency above is there for exactly that.
  • The solar range is wide — £9,600 to £16,400 — because it depends which trees we take down and how much sun the array actually gets. Two weeks of photographs settles it before anything is ordered.
  • Labour is genuinely hard to get. A large contractor was on GBN News this month importing workers from four countries.
  • Two measurements are still missing, both in the kitchen, and they hold up the cabinet order rather than the build.

What I'm actually asking

Not to back a venture. Whether the house Dad built stays in the family and gets used, or whether we let the weather finish it. If we do nothing, the honest end of that road is a sale.

Realistic start is April, and it isn't a long build — ten to fourteen weeks at Grenada pace, so the house is finished and standing well before Spicemas. I'd want the work to draw a season of visitors who can point at something and say they did that.

Fifteen thousand each. Once. It buys a share of the house, and nothing you'll ever be asked to top up.
