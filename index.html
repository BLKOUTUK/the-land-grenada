01 — The story

A return, two generations in the making.

My parents left Grenada in the 1960s, raised their family in London, came back to build a house in the 1990s, and returned to England again. The land waited. Now the youngest of that family has come home to it — to bring it back into use without selling what makes it matter.

Mum and Dad built the house from stone that was already on the land, salvaged from the old water mill and the Georgian houses at the heart of the village. The walls are half a metre thick. Hurricane Ivan, in 2004, took out the first build, and they started again. It is standing empty now, and the damp, the cracks and the weather are winning. Left as it is, the realistic end is a sale. This is the other ending.

02 — What it will be

A house to stay in, a kitchen that brings people in.

For around twenty weeks a year the house is let as one whole house, to one party — never room by room. The rest of the year it's the family's. Behind it, the old warehouse becomes a commercial kitchen, and the mango grove gets three guest suites, each sleeping up to six: a family, or a team.

The register is the neighbourhood's own gap: artists, dining, live music, diaspora and culture, under fairy lights. The food is seasonal — more fish and vegetables, less meat, less fried — traditional Caribbean cooking done well.

03 — The place & its moment

An east coast village, with more going on than you'd think.

Simon is in St Andrew, on the wet, green east coast — not the tourist strip. The sea is a seven-minute walk; the view from the house runs west to the hills, and the sunset comes down that line.

On the land

The stone house · the old warehouse · the mango grove · the mill ruin · the rest of the seven acres, farmed.

Within reach

Seamoon Industrial Park, in Simon itself, where a call centre employs about 800 people · the FIFA Technical Development Centre, being built · the Seamoon Cultural Centre, late but due to be complete for 2027.

The timing

The Seamoon Pavilion hosts the Soca Monarch semi-finals every year. At Spicemas 2026, visitors from overseas were up 24% on the year before.

What it doesn't touch

Everything stays inside the house and the mango grove. The mill and the rest of the land, farmed now, are not part of this.

04 — The plan

Small is all. The house first, then the business behind it, then what it earns.

The house is shared, and the business is mine. They're costed separately and kept apart, so the house never carries the business's risk. What the business adds later, it pays for from what it earns.

  1. PHASE 0The family restores the house. Roof, damp and cracks dealt with, with a structural engineer's view first. A full rewire, new plumbing and septic. One bathroom laid out for two parties at once, a new kitchen, skylights, and the back of the house opened to the land with a five-metre hurricane-rated glass opening. Solar on the roof. Let whole for about twenty weeks a year; the family's the rest. This is a proposal the family is talking through now.
  2. PHASE 1My business, in the back garden. The warehouse becomes a commercial kitchen, with a servery onto the garden for up to 30 and a takeaway window to the road. Two guest suites in the mango grove, from flat-pack modules around one shared yard. The warehouse's own solar, and e-scooters and trikes for guests and deliveries.
  3. PHASE 1BWhat it earns, and a crowdfunder. The third suite, the pergola and bar and, in year two, the pool.

The business costs £65,350 to £77,145 to build before it trades: the warehouse kitchen, the first two suites, import and landing, fees, and the warehouse's own solar with a backup generator. The third suite, the pergola and bar come later, from earnings and a crowdfunder.

I've modelled five years three ways. In the central case the business pays back its build in year 3. In the low case it doesn't pay back within five years. I'm showing both, because both are possible.

Be clear about what these numbers are worth. The build costs rest on real quotes, published prices and wage rates. The revenue is mostly judgement: a shape, not a forecast. The weakest number is how often the suites will be booked, and at what rate. The cheapest research still to do is to ask Grenadians what they'd pay for a supper-club seat and a Friday lunch.

Where the rate comes from. A suite costs US$100 a night for a couple off-peak (about £75, ≈ EC$270), plus US$25 for each extra guest, rising 5% a year. Carnival and July/August, about nine weeks a year, carry a 1.75× premium; the calculator uses the off-peak couple's rate all year, so it sits below the plan on purpose. Occupancy defaults to 34%, the average for short lets in Egmont, Grenada (33.5%); the low case takes 80% of that, the high case 120%. The build figure is the top of the range, £77,145 (low end £65,350).

The suites carry maintenance (9% of revenue), supplies & F&B (18%) and marketing (13% of room income); fixed running costs default to the plan's figure once the business is up to speed. Supper-club nights and the Friday lunch and takeaway figures are the plan's own low, central and high cases. The first two years ramp up to this, which the calculator doesn't show. Shown in £ and EC$ only (£1 = EC$3.60; EC$ pegged to the US dollar at 2.70).

Getting it built

From orders to carnival.

The builder is a Grenadian contractor whose quote we already hold. February is the start of the dry season, and a ten-to-fourteen-week build finishes by May, before the hurricane season.

The date that matters most is goods leaving China by mid January, before Chinese New Year closes the factories. Miss it, and everything slips by about two months. Some of this is outside our control: shipping and fuel costs are moving with world events, and the weather sets the building season.

The timetable

  • November 2026 — orders placed, builder's contract signed, planning applications in
  • Mid January — goods leave China
  • February 2027 — building starts
  • May — building finished; inspections and licences
  • June — trial: family and friends stay first
  • July–August — open for the carnival season; Spicemas 9–10 August
